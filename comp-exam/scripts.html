Slide 12Hypertension · Burden — Half of the adults with high blood pressure did not know it.3 clicks

The first leaf is burden. How common is hypertension, and do people know they have it?

1 Percy et al. 1997Percy [PUR-see] and colleagues wanted the first population-based picture of hypertension on the Navajo Nation, because older numbers came from hospitals and clinics. Their method was a random household survey: they picked areas, then households, at random across the eight Indian Health Service units, so the results stand for the whole adult population. They measured blood pressure three times and averaged the last two. Of 97 adults with high readings, only 50 had ever been told. This table shows the people who had been told: most got advice to lose weight or cut salt, and 61 percent were following it.

2 Sharlin et al. 1993Sharlin [SHAR-lin] and colleagues asked the same question with one Northern Plains tribe in South Dakota in 1986. Their method treats a person's answer like a screening test. They asked: has a health professional ever told you your blood pressure is high? Then they measured it. Sensitivity is the share of people with measured high blood pressure who said yes. Overall it was 50 percent. For adults 18 to 39, it was 27 percent.

3 → What I learned: knowing your own numbers …What I learned: knowing your own numbers is the first step of self-management. So measuring blood pressure belongs inside the tool.

Slide 13Hypertension · Burden — Urban Native elders and older Medicare patients carry a heavy load.3 clicks

Next, two studies of older Native adults.

1 Rhoades & Buchwald 2003Rhoades [ROADS] and Buchwald [BOOK-wald] wanted to study older urban American Indians and Alaska Natives, a group often left out of research. Their method was a chart review: a trained abstractor read the chart of every patient aged 50 and older seen in one year at an urban Indian clinic in Seattle, and compared the care with the national guideline of that time. 38 percent had a hypertension diagnosis. Most were on medicine, but only about a third were controlled. This table shows that lab tests were common, but nutrition and exercise counseling were recorded for only about one in four.

2 Eberly et al. 2023Eberly [EB-er-lee] and colleagues, working from Gallup Indian Medical Center, wanted current numbers, because the main heart studies of Native people were more than 20 years old. Their method was a national cohort built from Medicare billing records: about 220,000 American Indian and Alaska Native patients aged 66 and older, followed from 2015 to 2019. 72 percent had hypertension, and half had a serious heart condition. The maps show that these patients live across the whole country, not only on reservations.

3 → What I learned: medicine alone is not …What I learned: medicine alone is not enough. People need support for daily self-management where they live, in cities as well as on the reservation.

Slide 14Hypertension · Risk — Risk factors travel together.3 clicks

The second leaf is risk.

1 Mendlein et al. 1997Mendlein [MEND-line] and colleagues wanted to know whether heart disease, once rare among the Navajo, was likely to rise. They used the same 1991 to 1992 Navajo survey, with interviews in Navajo or English, blood tests, a glucose test, and body measurements for 788 adults. 35 percent of men and 62 percent of women were overweight, and obese adults were about four times as likely to have diabetes, hypertension, or a poor cholesterol level. The authors say diet and physical activity hold the most promise for prevention.

2 Jernigan et al. 2010Jernigan [JUR-nih-gun] and colleagues wanted to see how heart risk changed nationally over ten years. Their method was a repeated cross-sectional survey: two national phone surveys, ten years apart, with different people each time, adjusted so that age and education did not explain the change. In the figure, 79 percent had at least one of six risk factors, and the share with three or more grew. The authors recommend community-based participatory research with Native communities, which I come back to later.

3 → What I learned: a blood-pressure tool should …What I learned: a blood-pressure tool should also touch diet, activity, and weight, because these risks act together.

Slide 15Hypertension · Risk — Hypertension is the largest single share of heart disease.3 clicks

Which risk factor matters most?

1 Rawal et al. 2023Rawal [rah-WAHL] and colleagues used the American Heart Association's Life's Simple 7: smoking, activity, weight, diet, blood pressure, cholesterol, and glucose. Their method combined a national survey of about 8,500 Native adults with a population attributable fraction, which estimates the share of all heart disease linked to one factor. In this figure, hypertension is the highest: 42 percent. People with mostly ideal scores had 80 percent lower odds of heart disease.

2 Zamora-Kapoor et al. 2024Zamora-Kapoor [zah-MOR-ah kah-POOR] and colleagues asked whether body weight is the pathway from teenage habits to adult blood pressure. Their method was mediation analysis: test each risk factor with and without BMI in the model; if the effect shrinks, weight is the pathway. They followed about 7,800 adolescents, including 312 American Indian and Alaska Native youth. The Native youth had the highest obesity, smoking, and later high blood pressure, 27 percent. But with only 312 people, no adjusted factor was significant, so the authors ask future studies to oversample Native youth.

3 → What I learned: blood pressure is the …What I learned: blood pressure is the strongest place to start, and Native samples must be large enough to learn from.

Slide 16Hypertension · Context — Heart risk is clinical and social at the same time.3 clicks

The third leaf is context.

1 Breathett et al. 2020Breathett [BREH-thet] and colleagues wrote the American Heart Association's scientific statement on heart health in American Indians and Alaska Natives. A scientific statement is an expert narrative review: a writing group brings together the most relevant studies, here mostly from the Strong Heart Study, into guidance for clinicians. Their figure shows the problem as clinical risks, like diabetes and hypertension, plus social determinants, like income, insurance, toxic metals, and discrimination. In 2017, the Indian Health Service spent 4,078 dollars per person, compared with 13,185 for Medicare.

2 Assari & Zare 2025Assari [ah-SAH-ree] and Zare [zah-REH] tested a pattern called Minorities' Diminished Returns: the same resource, like a college degree, protects marginalized groups less. Their method was regression with a race-by-education interaction, using the 2023 National Health Interview Survey. More education went with lower odds of hypertension, but less so for American Indian and Alaska Native adults.

3 → What I learned: knowledge alone will not …What I learned: knowledge alone will not lower blood pressure. Design has to work with access, cost, and history.

Slide 17Hypertension · Care at Home — Care can come to the home.3 clicks

The fourth leaf is care at home. Two trials bring blood-pressure care to where people live.

1 Hózhó trial (Eberly et al.)Eberly's [EB-er-lee] team at Gallup Indian Medical Center saw that Diné patients with heart failure often were not on the four recommended medicines, because specialists are far away and primary care providers lack time. The Hózhó [hoh-ZHOH] trial was designed with community members and providers. Its method is a stepped-wedge randomized trial: patients are split into five groups, and each group starts the program 30 days after the one before, so everyone gets it, and the timing still allows a fair comparison. Each patient gets a home blood-pressure cuff, and a team adds missing medicines and adjusts doses by phone. The file in my corpus is the protocol, so I report the design, not results.

2 Sinclair et al. 2023 (NOSH)Sinclair [SIN-klair] and colleagues saw that the DASH diet had never been tested with Native participants. NOSH is a randomized trial with a waitlist control, planned for 370 urban American Indian and Alaska Native adults at three urban Indian clinics. The control group gets the program after the study, so no one goes without it. The program is eight weekly phone or Zoom sessions with a dietitian on DASH and traditional foods, plus 30 dollars of groceries a week. They also built in low-literacy supports, like hand pictures for portion sizes.

3 → What I learned: home cuffs, phone support, …What I learned: home cuffs, phone support, and simple materials lower the barriers of distance and cost.

Slide 19Disability · Disability and BP — Blood pressure sits inside the disability experience.3 clicks

The first disability leaf: disability and blood pressure.

1 Hodge 1989Hodge [HODJ] wanted tribes to have their own data for planning rehabilitation services, because almost none existed. Her method was a needs assessment built with three tribal vocational rehabilitation programs: Navajo, Chippewa-Cree [CHIP-uh-wah KREE], and Shoshone-Bannock [shoh-SHOH-nee BAN-uk]. The tribal programs helped write the questions, and local staff asked them, in the office, to 170 clients. In her table, high blood pressure is the second most common health problem, at 31.8 percent, and 42 percent among Navajo clients. 84 percent lived with family, and relatives gave rides and care.

2 Sanderson et al. 1998Sanderson [SAN-der-sun] and colleagues, at NAU's American Indian Rehabilitation Research and Training Center, interviewed 32 American Indians with disabilities in northwest New Mexico, three quarters of them Navajo. A needs assessment asks people what they use, what they need, and what they could not get. Blindness, hearing loss, and hypertension were the most common disabilities. People preferred family members as helpers, and asked for information through local radio, tribal newspapers, and tribal events.

3 → What I learned: ask people directly, involve …What I learned: ask people directly, involve family, and share information through channels the community already uses.

Slide 20Disability · Place — The place can disable, and community can protect.3 clicks

The second disability leaf is place.

1 Reisinger & Ripat 2014Reisinger [RYE-sing-er] and Ripat [RIP-at] wanted to know what makes assistive technology work, or fail, on the Navajo Nation. Their method was community-based participatory research with ASSIST! to Independence, a Navajo-owned independent living center. They held talking circles, a culturally familiar focus group where each person speaks in turn, in Navajo or English, in Chinle [chin-LEE]. 28 users of wheelchairs, prostheses, hearing aids, and other devices took part. Their model shows a cycle: lack of awareness, not understanding, and not feeling understood. Users said roads, water, and housing disabled them more than their bodies did, and wheelchairs built for city sidewalks got stuck in sand and mud.

2 Mashinchi et al. 2022Mashinchi [mah-SHEEN-chee] and colleagues looked at disability by community, not by person. Their method was a county-level analysis of Census data for all 3,220 U.S. counties, mapped against tribal lands. In counties where at least 5 percent of people are Native, Native disability was 16.4 percent, compared with 21 to 24 percent elsewhere. They suggest that belonging and culture may protect.

3 → What I learned: design and test at …What I learned: design and test at home, in the person's language, and treat community as a strength.

Slide 21Disability · Native Views — Many Native languages have no word for disability.3 clicks

The third disability leaf is Native views of disability. These are conceptual papers, and they changed how I think about design.

1 Weaver 2015Weaver [WEE-ver], a social work scholar, reviewed how Native American cultures understand disability. Many Native languages have no word for disability. Many Native cultures value interdependence more than independence, and a person who fills a role in their family is often not seen as disabled at all. She also shows that colonization, through boarding schools, reservations, and forced sterilization, produced disability.

2 Frankland et al. 2004Frankland [FRANK-lund] and colleagues, including Lavine Blackmountain of Navajo Nation Vocational Rehabilitation, asked whether the special education idea of self-determination fits Diné families. Their method was a conceptual review comparing the four parts of self-determination with Diné values. This figure shows the term's two roots: a nation's political right, and a person's psychological growth. They found each part matters to Diné families, but it is lived through family, clan, and interdependence. The Diné term is tááweeajitéégó [TAH-weh-ah-jih-TAY-goh]. Traditional Diné families expect every child, with or without a disability, to contribute to the family.

3 → What I learned: "helping my family" may …What I learned: in a game for Diné families, helping my family may motivate more than doing it on my own.

Slide 22Disability · Native Views — Self-determination, and K'é: relatives first.3 clicks

Two more papers on Native views.

1 Rabang et al. 2023Rabang [rah-BAHNG] and colleagues, mostly Indigenous scholars, including Vanessa Hiratsuka [hee-raht-SOO-kah], a Navajo Nation member, wrote a position paper using Indigenous storytelling and program cases. They show two meanings of self-determination: a disabled person making their own choices, and a tribal nation governing itself. They argue one is not possible without the other. In their example, Oyáte [oh-YAH-tay] Circle in South Dakota, people trusted materials more when they carried the program's own Lakota branding instead of the university's.

2 Yellowhorse 2018, 2022Yellowhorse [YEL-oh-horse], a Diné scholar and parent of a disabled child, wrote a master's thesis in 2018 and a paper in 2022. Her method is critical analysis. She visited a Navajo immersion school whose building followed Diné teachings, yet students still moved away from their autistic classmates. Her point: adding Diné language and design on the surface is not enough if the system underneath still sorts children. She proposes K'é [k'EH, with a popped k], Diné kinship, so that every child is first a relative. In 2022 she showed how COVID responses valued people by productivity, and offered a Diné way of valuing people through relationship.

3 → What I learned: culture must shape the …What I learned: culture must shape a game's rules and goals, not only its pictures and words.

Slide 23Disability · Measures — Standard tests need to be read with Diné home life in mind.3 clicks

The last disability leaf is measures. Two papers from the Navajo Birth Cohort Study.

1 Nozadi et al. 2019Nozadi [noh-ZAH-dee] and colleagues noticed that programs on the Navajo Nation use a baby screening tool whose national norms included only about 1 percent Native children. Navajo staff visited 530 infants at home. The team compared scores with national norms, and, because there is no gold standard diagnosis, used a Bayesian method to estimate the tool's accuracy. At 10 months, the tool flagged 32 percent of Navajo babies in gross motor skills, compared with 5 percent nationally. The authors point to home life: many adults hold and help the baby, and cradle boards, not only ability.

2 Rennie et al. 2023Rennie [REN-ee] and colleagues followed the same cohort to about age 4. Each of 138 Diné children had a three-to-four-hour visit with many tests, parent reports, and a team diagnosis, with tools reviewed by traditional healers and community members. In their figure, language scores were low in every group, while nonverbal and spatial scores were average. They caution against over-labeling Diné children, and also against missing children who need help.

Slide 29Theory · Serious Games — What makes a serious game for health?3 clicks

The first theory leaf is serious games. What makes a serious game for health?

1 Clochesy et al. 2015Clochesy [KLOH-shee] and colleagues wanted to help people with chronic illness talk better with their providers. A serious game entertains, but it is built for a health goal. They built eSMART-HD, a 3D game where players practice a clinic visit with avatar staff and a coach. Their method was a community participatory framework: eleven community focus groups, then a script tested with actors, then avatars, shown in these six steps. Captions were first added for people with hearing loss, but the team saw that captions helped every user, so captions became the default.

2 Elements of a serious gameTheir table maps each element of a serious game to a design choice, using Lieberman's [LEE-ber-mun] model: challenge, motivation, role models, simulation, repetition, and feedback. For example, a coach avatar, and levels where the coaching becomes less each time.

1 Hickman et al. 2021Hickman [HIK-mun] and colleagues tested eSMART-HTN, a virtual clinic visit where adults with hypertension practice talking with an avatar doctor and a coach. Real steps move the avatar up a trail to Lumbee landmarks, and coins buy cultural items, like the pinecone patchwork shown here. Slide 36Theory · Wicked Problems · The Ten Characteristics — Rittel and Webber's ten characteristics of a wicked problem.3 clicks

What makes a problem wicked? Graham [GRAY-um] and colleagues used Rittel [RIT-ul] and Webber's [WEB-er] ten characteristics.

1 No definitive formulation : you cannot fully define …First: there is no definitive formulation, so you cannot fully define the problem. There is no stopping rule, so you never know when you are done. Solutions are good or bad, not true or false. There is no immediate or final test of a solution. And every try is a one-shot operation, with real consequences.

2 No full list of solutions : there is …Next: there is no full list of possible solutions. Every wicked problem is unique. Each one is a symptom of another problem. How you explain the problem decides how you solve it. And the designer has no right to be wrong, because they are responsible for the results.

3 → Daily blood-pressure care fits: it has no …Daily blood pressure care fits this. It has no end point, and what works differs from family to family. So a health game cannot be solved once. It is designed, tried, and changed.

Slide 55Backup · Data Governance Frameworks — CARE, FAIR, OCAP, and the 4 R's: who decides about Indigenous data?6 clicks

the slideHow can Indigenous communities set rules for their data, and for the research relationship? Four frameworks help. (click) CARE, from Carroll and colleagues, has four principles: collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility, and ethics. It asks: who has the right to decide about this data, and who benefits? (click) FAIR asks a different question: how should data be managed so others can find and reuse it? Findable, accessible, interoperable, and reusable. (click) OCAP [OH-cap] was made by First Nations in Canada: ownership, control, access, and possession. (click) The 4 R's, from Kirkness [KURK-ness] and Barnhardt [BARN-hart], are about how researchers work with a community: respect, relevance, reciprocity, and responsibility. (click) They work together. CARE and OCAP come first: the community decides if and how data is shared. FAIR comes second, only inside those limits. FAIR without CARE can go against a tribe's sovereignty. And the 4 R's guide how we work with the Diné community the whole time. (click) What this means for design: the Diné community decides what is shared, then FAIR keeps the shared data usable. This links to RQ2, and to PRQ3 later.

Slide 56Backup · Cultural Property — Culture belongs to the community: what to share, and how to show strengths.5 clicks

the slideTwo more papers add to cultural grounding. They are about what to share, and how. (click) Belarde-Lewis [beh-LAR-day LOO-iss], a Zuni [ZOO-nee] and Tlingit [KLING-kit] scholar, wrote about cultural intellectual property. Songs, ceremonies, and masks are easy to take once someone puts them online. (click) For example, Zuni and Hopi communities do not allow photos or recordings of ceremonies. At the Tribal Journeys canoe event, the Ahousaht [uh-HOW-saht] people asked visitors not to post their ancestor masks on the internet.
