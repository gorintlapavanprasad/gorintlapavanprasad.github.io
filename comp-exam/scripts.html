Problem

Slide 1Playful Health Technology for Hypertension Management at the Intersection of Indigeneity and Disability

Good morning, and thank you for being here.

My comprehensive exam is a scoping review of playful health technology for hypertension self-management in Indigenous communities: hypertension care where Indigeneity and disability meet.

Slide 2Land Acknowledgement — Northern Arizona University sits at the base of the San Francisco Peaks, on homelands sacred to Native peoples of this region.

Before I begin, I want to acknowledge the land. [Read slowly.]

This matters for this work, because the people at its center are Native people living in Flagstaff, on this land.

Slide 3What is hypertension?

First, what is hypertension? It is blood pressure that stays high, 130 over 80 or above. It usually has no symptoms, so it is often called the silent killer, yet over years it damages the heart, kidneys, and brain.

How many people? About 1.28 billion adults worldwide, roughly one in three aged 30 to 79; in the US, nearly half of adults. And close to half do not know they have it.

The hopeful part: it is manageable. Even a 10 mmHg drop in systolic pressure meaningfully lowers risk. Because control comes from daily behavior, this review examines playful, everyday technology as one form of daily support.

Slide 4For urban Navajo (Diné) adults, it is under-recognized.

I narrow from Indigenous people in general to the community this work is with: urban Navajo, or Diné, adults in Flagstaff.

Self-reported hypertension, through the Navajo Epidemiology Center, is about 28 percent, near the US average. But measured studies run higher, and in Percy's Navajo survey only 50 of 97 adults with high blood pressure had ever been told, so it is under-recognized. Heart disease was the fourth leading cause of death on the New Mexico Navajo Nation from 2021 to 2024.

Slide 5People with disabilities carry more hypertension, too.

The other dimension is disability, and I treat people with disabilities broadly, not as a subgroup of any one community.

Hypertension runs roughly double the general rate: 42.5 percent of US adults with a disability, 56.4 percent of adults with a physical disability in one Korean study, and 63.9 percent of Deaf adults. And it is harder to manage, because materials are often inaccessible, care can depend on a proxy, and almost no tools are built for disabled players.

Slide 6Where Indigeneity and disability meet.

This is where my framing comes in: intersectional research in HCI. The usual approach takes two populations, Navajo people and people with disabilities, and studies them side by side. Crenshaw showed why that misses people: where identities meet, the experience is different, not added.

(click) So this review treats them as one population: urban Diné adults with disabilities, and the people who support them, in Flagstaff. HCI has mostly studied one identity at a time, so studying both together is a contribution the field has been asking for.

Slide 7Research Questions

Three research questions organize the review.

(click) RQ1 asks how serious games and playful health technologies are used for hypertension self-management. (click) RQ1.1 narrows that same body of work: what portion is actually designed for Indigenous communities and for people with disabilities. (click) RQ2 turns to method: what human-centered design approaches support intersectional design of these tools with both communities.

Scoping Review

Slide 8Finding the Literature

I searched three databases on 7 September 2026, using six concept blocks: games, technology, hypertension, Indigenous, disability, and design. PubMed gave 1,539 records, the ACM Digital Library 100, and Google Scholar 120, 1,759 in all.

After removing 50 duplicates, I screened 1,709 titles and abstracts, read 102 in full, and 67 met the inclusion criteria.

Slide 9A Hierarchical Thematic Analysis — 67 studies, coded and arranged into a hierarchy.

To organize the 67 studies, I did a hierarchical thematic analysis. I coded every paper for its themes, population, self-management factors, form of play, and design approach, and arranged the themes into this hierarchy. The numbers are papers.

On the left is domain knowledge: hypertension, with its five factors and risk; and intersectionality, with a disability branch and an Indigenous branch. Only one study sits in both. On the right is theory: serious games and play; participatory, community-based research; and wicked problems with research through design.

This hierarchy is the structure of the rest of this talk. For each part, I show what the literature says, the design implications, and a great example of prior work.

Background

Slide 10Hypertension is managed through five daily behaviors.

Hypertension is managed through five daily behaviors. (click) Diet. (click) Exercise. (click) Medication. (click) Blood-pressure monitoring. (click) Education. Lifestyle change is first-line care, and even a 10 mmHg drop in systolic pressure lowers cardiovascular risk. The review charts the literature by these five factors.

Slide 11Hypertension · Risk — Weight and sitting raise the risk, and one form of sitting is play.

What raises the risk? In 833 adults with disabilities, weight mattered most: obesity raised the odds almost 7 times, and age 65+ about 2.5 times. That sample was in Taiwan, so I use it as cross-population evidence. And in teenagers, seated video gaming was the only sitting habit linked to higher blood pressure, likely through snacking and the stress of play.

Design implications: move players during play, rather than seat them; and help people find out their numbers, because many do not know.

The example is local: Percy and colleagues at the Navajo Area IHS measured blood pressure in 780 Navajo adults. Only 50 of the 97 people with high blood pressure had been told.

Slide 12The literature narrows to an empty center.

This is the heart of the gap, and I build it up one condition at a time.

(click) There is a growing body of serious games and playful technology for hypertension. (click) A small number are designed with an Indigenous community. (click) Fewer still with people with disabilities. (click) And no study brings all four together: a game, for hypertension, with an Indigenous community, and with disability, as one population. (click) That empty center is the gap this review makes visible.

Slide 13Disability · Proxy Care — A caregiver can scaffold a player, or quietly stand in for them.

The first disability theme is proxy care. People with disabilities often take part with a caregiver. That can help: in Caro's exergame study, the help adults needed dropped from 14.4 times in the first session to 2.7 in the third. But a proxy can also speak for the person, Sum warns about proxies used as substitutes under the guise of inclusion.

Design implications: the supporter scaffolds, and the player decides. And partial participation, like pressing the cuff button, counts as real participation.

The example I learn most from is Bircanin and colleagues. Over two years they co-designed with adults with severe intellectual disabilities using Active Support: little and often, only as much help as needed, and choice stays with the person.

Slide 14Disability · Accessible Protocols — The method itself often leaves disabled participants out.

The second disability theme is accessible research protocols. A 2026 review of 25 co-design studies with people with intellectual disability found only 7 used accessibility standards like Easy Read.

Design implications: easy-read materials, short and frequent sessions, familiar places, and fair pay.

A great example is Spencer González and colleagues. For three years they worked with an advisory panel of adults with intellectual disability, paid like staff. They used easy-read text, adult pictures rather than childish ones, story-based play, and polished prototypes, because rough wireframes confused people.

Slide 15Disability · Technosolutionism — Most accessible-tech work still treats disability as a deficit to fix.

The third disability theme is technosolutionism: the belief that a new technology will fix the problem. Hassan's 2024 review of game accessibility makes the pattern concrete. Of 162 studies, only 13 drew on disability-studies theory; the rest framed disability as a deficit to fix. Sum and colleagues name the technosolutionist drive that casts disabled and underserved people as problems in need of intervention.

Design implications: confirm the problem is real for the community, and treat opting out as a real choice, not a failure.

The study I point to is Ymous and colleagues, 2020. Seven disabled scholars describe the epistemic violence of being written up as burdens in the research about them. Their title says it: I am just terrified of my future.

Slide 16Disability · DREEM — Learn before asking people for their time.

The last disability theme is a method from our lab: DREEM. It says learn first, from media made by disabled creators, before asking anyone to give their time. That avoids spending participants' access labor, the extra work disabled people do just to take part, and it pushes back on saviorism.

For my work: start with media made by Diné creators and by disabled creators, then bring better questions to the community.

Here are its four steps: discover media, read it closely, keep a journal, and aggregate the insights.

Slide 17Indigenous · Sovereignty — Data sovereignty is rarely addressed.

Now the Indigenous branch. First, sovereignty. A critical review by Vigil-Hayes and colleagues found only 4 of 84 Indigenous health-technology studies engaged with data sovereignty: who owns and controls the community's data. And historical trauma, from colonization and harmful research, shapes the trust any study depends on.

Design implications: the community controls its data, following the CARE principles, and results go back. And a community advisory board guides the questions and approaches.

The example is Maar and colleagues, from DREAM-GLOBAL with First Nations communities. This figure is how they built and kept research relationships before, during, and after the trial. 98 percent of participants would recommend the program.

Slide 18Indigenous · Cultural Grounding — Programs work when they build on culture.

The second Indigenous theme is cultural grounding: programs work when built into what a community already does. The strongest evidence is the hula trial with 263 Native Hawaiian adults: 42.7 percent reached healthy blood pressure, against 21.2. I use it because it is the only randomized trial of a cultural movement practice for blood pressure. The lesson is the approach, not the dance. Navajo Healthy Stores, run by local Navajo-speaking health workers, shows the same.

Design implications: start from what the community values, and the community decides what is shared, because some games and stories are only for certain seasons.

The great local example is ARORA, by Vigil-Hayes and colleagues at NAU, with Navajo and Hopi youth in Flagstaff. An advisory board set the design values: the butterfly as a positive symbol, and no coyote or lightning.

Slide 19Serious Games and Play — Most health games are closed systems.

On the theory side, first: serious games and play. A serious game is a game with a health aim besides fun. Of the 22 games and playful tools for hypertension in this review, nearly all are closed, structured games or simple gamification; open, situated play is rare. The trials are mixed: eSMART-HD, where adults practice a medical visit, lowered systolic pressure about 7 points in the game group, but the difference from control was not significant and the study was small. MyHeartMate kept only 27 percent of its players after 30 days.

Design implications: leave room for open, social play that fits daily life, and design for engagement that lasts.

The framework comes from Duval: serious games for health are closed systems; serious play stays open, so people can shape it to their own lives.

Slide 20Hypertension &amp; Heart Games Compared —

Here are the hypertension and heart games side by side. eSMART-HD, with 116 adults, lowered systolic about 7 mmHg in the game group, but the trial was small and not significant vs control. MyHeartMate, with 390 heart patients, was used by almost everyone at first, but only 27 percent kept going after 30 days. Stamm asked 11 older adults what an exercise game should be: safe, wireless, controllers. The Lumbee game is the only one built with a Native tribe, and it excludes people who use a walker. Graham's workshop, from our lab, produced 5 prototypes. And Li found adults with prehypertension wanted simple, social play.

What is missing across all of them: players with disabilities, and lasting engagement.

Slide 21Participatory, Community-Based Research — Few studies design with the community.

Second: participatory, community-based research. About 13 studies designed with the community, 7 with Indigenous communities and 6 with people with disabilities, and the two sets barely overlap. And Harrington warns that design workshops can themselves be an affluent and privileged activity.

Design implications: pay people, value what the community already has, let the community set the goals, and stay after the funding ends.

The example is the Lumbee game trial, the one hypertension game built with a Native tribe. A paid community committee shaped it, and removed a casino-themed game. But it excludes people who use a walker or have sensory impairments, which is exactly the gap for this population.

Slide 22Wicked Problems and Research through Design — A health game is a wicked problem, so you learn by building.

Third: wicked problems and research through design. No paper in my search used these words, so this comes from the theory literature. Duval calls designing a motivating health game a wicked problem: no single definition, and answers are better or worse, not right or wrong. Research through design answers this by learning through making.

Design implications: find the play people already do and build on it; and treat the platform as a probe to learn with, as well as a product.

The example is Situated Play Design. In this workshop, food traditions from many cultures became play potentials, starting points for playful design.

Synthesis

Slide 23Where Each Concern Was Studied — Each concern stays inside one community.

This table shows something that only appears when the two groups are looked at together. Each row is a theme; the columns show how many studies addressed it with Indigenous communities, with people with disabilities, and with general populations.

Cultural grounding and sovereignty were studied almost only with Indigenous communities. Proxy care and accessible protocols were studied only with people with disabilities. No study looked at proxy care or accessible protocols with Indigenous people. Each concern stays inside one community, which is the gap an intersectional review is meant to show.

Slide 24Synthesis Matrix — Nothing does all of it, and three columns are nearly empty.

This synthesis matrix brings the great examples together. Each row is a study; each column is something a tool for this community needs. A check means the study does it.

Read down the columns. Hypertension and play are common, and the Indigenous studies are built with the community. But disability is almost empty wherever Indigenous is full. Data control and open play are mostly empty. Nothing does all of it, but many do some. The last row is where CardioCare Quest is heading.

(Click.) The three thin columns, disability, data control, and open play, are where the literature has the least to offer, and where this work has to contribute.

Slide 25From Themes to Design Requirements — Each requirement traces to the reviewed evidence.

Each design requirement comes from the themes, not from a list I made up. From the five factors and risk: move players during play, and calm them for measuring. From intersectionality, proxy care, and accessible protocols: design with players with disabilities, with support that fades. From sovereignty and cultural grounding: culturally grounded, led by the community, with community control of data. From games, community-based research, and wicked problems: open, situated play, built and studied as a probe with the community.

Slide 26Preliminary Research Questions — Four questions, from the guiding questions and requirements.

From the guiding questions and these requirements come four preliminary research questions. PRQ1, design: how do urban Diné adults with disabilities and their supporters co-design playful tools for daily blood-pressure care? PRQ2, support: how can caregiver help be designed in, while keeping the player's own voice? PRQ3, culture and sovereignty: how can cultural grounding and community data control be built in, and judged by the community? PRQ4, play: how do closed games and open, situated play differ in keeping people engaged?

Slide 27Limitations

The limitations. A scoping review maps the literature; it does not appraise study quality or pool effects. I searched three databases, in English, once, so some work is missed. Screening and coding were by a single reviewer, so the hierarchy is one defensible reading. And the counts show where attention sits, not what works.

Proposed Work

Slide 28Preliminary Work, in Order — What I have already done toward these questions.

My preliminary work, in the order it happened. First, a cultural probe: 31 Navajo adults, for 7 days, in English and Diné bizaad, with Native Americans for Community Action. Then a design session: 36 adults with disabilities and their caregivers created 23 game concepts. Then a play session: community members played CardioCare Quest, with and without support. Both papers are under review.

Slide 29CardioCare Quest Today — A game for each factor, moving for exercise, still for measuring.

What is CardioCare Quest today? A game for each factor: Dog Walking for exercise, DASH Diet for diet, Heart Smarts for education, Bingo Bash for medication, where a dose can be marked caregiver-assisted, and Vascular Village for measuring, which uses slow breathing before a calm, seated reading. That covers both sides: moving for exercise, still for measuring.

On the left is the in-app catalog, one game per factor; on the right is the Dog Walking exercise game, a GPS-tracked walk. It is built in Flutter and Firebase, with story games in Twine, and it works offline. It is not deployed yet; the next steps are accessibility work with players with disabilities, and a data plan with the community.

Slide 30What This Review Adds — One study designed with both.

What the review adds. Across the 67 studies, only one designed technology with both an Indigenous community and attention to disability: Graham and colleagues, 2025, from this lab, shown here. None paired that with a game for hypertension.

The review contributes an intersectional synthesis of the 67 studies, a hierarchy of themes with design implications, and design requirements traced to that evidence.

Future

Slide 31Future Work — From the Hozhoni Gameorama to a game for every factor.

Future work. The co-design workshop at the Hozhoni Gameorama gave us 23 game ideas, from 36 adults with disabilities and their caregivers, and the play session added feedback on CardioCare Quest.

The next step is to develop a game for each of the five factors with the community, do the accessibility work with players with disabilities, put a data-sovereignty plan in place, and then evaluate in a user study.

Conclusion

Slide 32Conclusion — A mapped field, one empty intersection, and a way forward.

To conclude. Across 67 studies, playful technology for hypertension rarely reaches Indigenous communities or people with disabilities, and no study brings a game to both. The review turns that into design requirements traced to the evidence. And CardioCare Quest, with the Hozhoni studies, is a first step in that direction, designed with urban Diné adults with disabilities.
