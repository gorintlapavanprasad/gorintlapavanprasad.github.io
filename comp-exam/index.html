What is hypertension? Hypertension is the medical name for high blood pressure. It means the pressure of the blood inside the blood vessels stays higher than normal for a long time. Blood pressure is measured with two numbers. The upper number is systolic, the pressure when the heart beats. The lower number is diastolic, the pressure when the heart rests between beats.

(click) When systolic is below 120 and diastolic is below 80, blood pressure is normal.

(click) When systolic is 120 to 129 and diastolic is below 80, it is called elevated.

(click) When systolic is 130 to 139, or diastolic is 80 to 89, it is stage 1 hypertension.

(click) When systolic is 140 or higher, or diastolic is 90 or higher, it is stage 2 hypertension.

(click) So hypertension starts at 130 over 80, which is the threshold in the guideline.

(click) In 2016, Harmon and colleagues conducted a study of Navajo adults, and they noted that cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of non-accidental death among Navajo people.

(click) In 2023, Eberly and colleagues studied the Medicare records of about 220,000 American Indian and Alaska Native adults aged 65 and older, from 2015 to 2019, and they found that 72.2 percent of them had hypertension.

(click) In 2023, Rawal and colleagues studied 8,497 American Indian and Alaska Native adults, and they estimated how much heart disease and stroke was linked to each risk factor, hypertension was linked to 42 percent, which is the largest share of any factor.

Closed games or open play?

Closed game

The system sets the rules and measures performance, as in eSMART [1] and Heart Health Mountain [2].

Open play

Players can change the rules; play is built on what people already do in daily life.

Situated Play Design

Three steps: find the play that already happens, design to support it, and test it in daily life [3].

[1] Clochesy et al. 2015[2] Radhakrishnan et al. 2025[3] Altarriba Bertran et al. 2019

A closed game sets the rules and measures how the player performs. eSMART and Heart Health Mountain are closed games: the game decides what counts as success.

Open play leaves room for players to change the rules, and builds on what people already do.

Situated Play Design, by Altarriba Bertran, Duval and colleagues, has three steps: find the play that already happens in daily life, design something that supports it, and test it in daily life.
