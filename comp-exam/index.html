The first leaf is burden: how common hypertension is, and whether people know they have it.

(click) Percy [PUR-see] and colleagues wanted the first population-based picture of hypertension on the Navajo Nation, because older numbers came from hospitals and clinics. Their method was a random household survey: they picked areas, then households, at random across the eight Indian Health Service units, so the results stand for the whole adult population. They measured blood pressure three times and averaged the last two. Of 97 adults with high readings, only 50 had ever been told. This table shows the people who had been told: 70 percent got advice, such as losing weight or cutting salt, and 61 percent were following it.

(click) Sharlin [SHAR-lin] and colleagues asked the same question with one Northern Plains tribe in South Dakota in 1986. Their method treats a person's answer like a screening test. They asked whether a health professional had ever said their blood pressure was high, and then they measured it. Sensitivity is the share of people with measured high blood pressure who said yes. Overall it was 50 percent. For adults 18 to 39, it was 27 percent.

(click) What I learned: knowing your own numbers is the first step of self-management. So measuring blood pressure belongs inside the tool.

Next, two studies of older Native adults.

(click) Rhoades [ROADS] and Buchwald [BOOK-wald] wanted to study older urban American Indians and Alaska Natives, a group other researchers have called an invisible minority. Their method was a chart review: a trained abstractor read the chart of every patient aged 50 and older seen in one year at an urban Indian clinic in Seattle, and compared the care with the national guideline of that time. 38 percent had a hypertension diagnosis. Of these, 83 percent were on medicine and 36 percent were controlled. In this table, cholesterol tests were recorded for 91 percent, and nutrition and exercise counseling for 27 percent.

(click) Eberly [EB-er-lee] and colleagues, working from Gallup Indian Medical Center, wanted current numbers, because the main heart studies of Native people were more than 20 years old. Their method was a national cohort built from Medicare billing records: about 220,000 American Indian and Alaska Native patients aged 66 and older, followed from 2015 to 2019. 72 percent had hypertension, and half had a serious heart condition. The maps show these patients across the whole country, in cities as well as near reservations.

(click) What I learned: medicine alone is not enough. People need support for daily self-management where they live, in cities as well as on the reservation.

The second leaf is risk.

(click) Mendlein [MEND-line] and colleagues wanted to know whether heart disease, once rare among the Navajo, was likely to rise. They used the same 1991 to 1992 Navajo survey, with interviews in Navajo or English, blood tests, a glucose test, and body measurements for 788 adults. 35 percent of men and 62 percent of women were overweight, and obese adults were about four times as likely to have diabetes, hypertension, or a poor cholesterol level. The authors say diet and physical activity hold the most promise for prevention.

(click) Jernigan [JUR-nih-gun] and colleagues wanted to see how heart risk changed nationally over ten years. Their method was a repeated cross-sectional survey: two national phone surveys, ten years apart, with different people each time, adjusted so that age and education did not explain the change. In the figure, 79 percent had at least one of six risk factors, and the share with three or more grew. The authors recommend community-based participatory research with Native communities, which I come back to later.

(click) What I learned: a blood-pressure tool should also touch diet, activity, and weight, because these risks act together.

Two papers compare the risk factors.

(click) Rawal [rah-WAHL] and colleagues used the American Heart Association's Life's Simple 7: smoking, activity, weight, diet, blood pressure, cholesterol, and glucose. Their method combined a national survey of about 8,500 Native adults with a population attributable fraction, which estimates the share of all heart disease linked to one factor. In this figure, hypertension is the highest: 42 percent. People with mostly ideal scores had 80 percent lower odds of heart disease.

(click) Zamora-Kapoor [zah-MOR-ah kah-POOR] and colleagues asked whether body weight is the pathway from teenage habits to adult blood pressure. Their method was mediation analysis: test each risk factor with and without BMI in the model; if the effect shrinks, weight is the pathway. They followed about 7,800 adolescents, including 312 American Indian and Alaska Native youth. The Native youth had the highest obesity, smoking, and later high blood pressure, 27 percent. But with only 312 people, no adjusted factor was significant, so the authors ask future studies to oversample Native youth.

(click) What I learned: hypertension carries the largest attributable share, so the tool starts with blood pressure, and Native samples must be large enough to analyze on their own.

The third leaf is context.

(click) Breathett [BREH-thet] and colleagues wrote the American Heart Association's scientific statement on heart health in American Indians and Alaska Natives. A scientific statement is an expert narrative review: a writing group selects and summarizes studies, here drawn largely from the Strong Heart Study, into guidance for clinicians. Their figure shows the problem as clinical risks, like diabetes and hypertension, plus social determinants, like income, insurance, toxic metals, and discrimination. In 2017, the Indian Health Service spent 4,078 dollars per person, compared with 13,185 for Medicare.

(click) Assari [ah-SAH-ree] and Zare [zah-REH] tested a pattern called Minorities' Diminished Returns: the same resource, like a college degree, protects marginalized groups less. Their method was regression with a race-by-education interaction, using the 2023 National Health Interview Survey. More education went with lower odds of hypertension, but less so for American Indian and Alaska Native adults.

(click) What I learned: knowledge alone will not lower blood pressure. Design has to work with access, cost, and history.

The fourth leaf is care at home. Two trials bring blood-pressure care to where people live.

(click) Eberly's [EB-er-lee] team at Gallup Indian Medical Center saw that Diné patients with heart failure often were not on the four recommended medicines, because specialists are far away and primary care providers lack time. The Hózhó [hoh-ZHOH] trial was designed with community members and providers. Its method is a stepped-wedge randomized trial: patients are split into five groups, and each group starts the program 30 days after the one before, so everyone gets it, and the timing still allows a fair comparison. Each patient gets a home blood-pressure cuff, and a team adds missing medicines and adjusts doses by phone. The file in my corpus is the protocol, so I report the design rather than results.

(click) Sinclair [SIN-klair] and colleagues saw that the DASH diet had never been tested with Native participants. NOSH is a randomized trial with a waitlist control, planned for 370 urban American Indian and Alaska Native adults at three urban Indian clinics. The control group gets the program after the study, so no one goes without it. The program is eight weekly phone or Zoom sessions with a dietitian on DASH and traditional foods, plus 30 dollars of groceries a week. They also built in low-literacy supports, like hand pictures for portion sizes.

(click) What I learned: home cuffs, phone support, and simple materials lower the barriers of distance and cost.

The first disability leaf: disability and blood pressure.

(click) Hodge [HODJ] wanted tribes to have their own data for planning rehabilitation services, because almost none existed. Her method was a needs assessment built with three tribal vocational rehabilitation programs: Navajo, Chippewa-Cree [CHIP-uh-wah KREE], and Shoshone-Bannock [shoh-SHOH-nee BAN-uk]. The tribal programs helped write the questions, and local staff asked them, in the office, to 170 clients. In her table, high blood pressure is the second most common health problem, at 31.8 percent, and 42 percent among Navajo clients. 84 percent lived with family, and relatives gave rides and care.

(click) Sanderson [SAN-der-sun] and colleagues, at NAU's American Indian Rehabilitation Research and Training Center, interviewed 32 American Indians with disabilities in northwest New Mexico, three quarters of them Navajo. A needs assessment asks people what they use, what they need, and what they could not get. Blindness, hearing loss, and hypertension were the most common disabilities. People preferred family members as helpers, and asked for information through local radio, tribal newspapers, and tribal events.

(click) What I learned: ask people directly, involve family, and share information through channels the community already uses.

The second disability leaf is place.

(click) Reisinger [RYE-sing-er] and Ripat [RIP-at] wanted to know what makes assistive technology work, or fail, on the Navajo Nation. Their method was community-based participatory research with ASSIST! to Independence, a Navajo-owned independent living center. They held talking circles, a culturally familiar focus group where each person speaks in turn, in Navajo or English, in Chinle [chin-LEE]. 28 users of wheelchairs, prostheses, hearing aids, and other devices took part. Their model shows a cycle: lack of awareness, not understanding, and not feeling understood. Users said roads, water, and housing disabled them more than their bodies did, and wheelchairs built for city sidewalks got stuck in sand and mud.

(click) Mashinchi [mah-SHEEN-chee] and colleagues looked at disability by community rather than by person. Their method was a county-level analysis of Census data for all 3,220 U.S. counties, mapped against tribal lands. In counties where at least 5 percent of people are Native, Native disability was 16.4 percent, compared with 21 to 24 percent elsewhere. They suggest that belonging and culture may protect.

(click) What I learned: design and test at home, in the person's language, and treat community as a strength.

The third disability leaf is Native views of disability. These are conceptual papers about how Native and Diné cultures understand disability.

(click) Weaver [WEE-ver], a social work scholar, reviewed how Native American cultures understand disability. She writes that most Native languages have no word equal to disability. She contrasts the mainstream U.S. value of independence with the value of interdependence in the Native cultures she reviews, where a person who fills a role in their family is often not seen as disabled. She also shows that colonization, through boarding schools, reservations, and forced sterilization, produced disability.

(click) Frankland [FRANK-lund] and colleagues, including Lavine Blackmountain of Navajo Nation Vocational Rehabilitation, asked whether the special education idea of self-determination fits Diné families. Their method was a conceptual review comparing the four parts of self-determination with Diné values. This figure shows the term's two roots: a nation's political right, and a person's psychological growth. They found each part matters to Diné families, but it is lived through family, clan, and interdependence. The Diné term is tááweeajitéégó [TAH-weh-ah-jih-TAY-goh]. Traditional Diné families expect every child, with or without a disability, to contribute to the family.

Wicked Problems · The Ten Characteristics

Rittel and Webber's ten characteristics of a wicked problem.

  1. No definitive formulation: you cannot fully define the problem
  2. No stopping rule: you never know when you are done
  3. Good or bad, not true or false: solutions are judged, not proven
  4. No immediate or final test: effects keep showing up over time
  5. Every try is a one-shot operation: each attempt has real consequences
  1. No full list of solutions: there is always another option
  2. Every wicked problem is unique: what worked elsewhere may not fit
  3. A symptom of another problem: problems sit inside other problems
  4. The explanation decides the solution: how you frame it shapes what you do
  5. No right to be wrong: designers are responsible for the results
→
Daily blood-pressure care fits: it has no end point, and what works differs from family to family. So a health game cannot be solved once; it is designed, tried, and changed.
[1]Rittel & Webber, 1973
