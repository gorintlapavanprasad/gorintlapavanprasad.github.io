First, what is hypertension? It is blood pressure that stays high, 130 over 80 or above. It usually has no symptoms, so it is often called the silent killer, yet over years it damages the heart, kidneys, and brain.

How many people? About 1.28 billion adults worldwide, roughly one in three aged 30 to 79; in the US, nearly half of adults. And close to half do not know they have it.

The hopeful part: it is manageable. Even a 10 mmHg drop in systolic pressure meaningfully lowers risk. Because control comes from daily behavior, this review examines playful, everyday technology as one form of daily support.

Where Indigeneity and disability meet.

These two are usually studied apart. Where they meet, the experience is different, not added (Crenshaw), and HCI has mostly studied one identity at a time.

The overlap is this review: urban Diné adults with disabilities, and their supporters, as one population.

Crenshaw, 1991 · Schlesinger et al., 2017 · Sum et al., 2022

This is where my framing comes in: intersectional research in HCI. The usual approach takes two populations, Navajo people and people with disabilities, and studies them side by side. Crenshaw showed why that misses people: where identities meet, the experience is different, not added.

(click) So this review treats them as one population: urban Diné adults with disabilities, and the people who support them, in Flagstaff. HCI has mostly studied one identity at a time, so studying both together is a contribution the field has been asking for.

Lin et al. 2012 · Goldfield et al. 2011 · Percy et al. 1997

What raises the risk? In 833 adults with disabilities, weight mattered most: obesity raised the odds almost 7 times, and age 65+ about 2.5 times. That sample was in Taiwan, so I use it as cross-population evidence. And in teenagers, seated video gaming was the only sitting habit linked to higher blood pressure, likely through snacking and the stress of play.

Design implications: move players during play, rather than seat them; and help people find out their numbers, because many do not know.

Two years co-designing with adults with severe intellectual disability: little and often, only the help needed, choice kept with the person. One participant, Merry, entered the activity room for the first time in years.

→

Caro et al. 2018 · Sum et al. 2024 · Bircanin et al. 2021

The first disability theme is proxy care. People with disabilities often take part with a caregiver. That can help: in Caro's exergame study, the help adults needed dropped from 14.4 times in the first session to 2.7 in the third. But a proxy can also speak for the person, Sum warns about proxies used as substitutes under the guise of inclusion.

Design implications: the supporter scaffolds, and the player decides. And partial participation, like pressing the cuff button, counts as real participation.

The example I learn most from is Bircanin and colleagues. Over two years they co-designed with adults with severe intellectual disabilities using Active Support: little and often, only as much help as needed, and choice stays with the person.

The method itself often leaves disabled participants out.

Three years with a paid advisory panel of adults with intellectual disability, treated as experts by experience: easy-read text, adult (not childish) images, story-based activities, and polished prototypes rather than rough sketches.

→
Adapt the materials, meet in familiar places, keep sessions short, and pay people for their time.
Johnstone et al. 2026 · Harrington et al. 2019 · Spencer González et al. 2020

The second disability theme is accessible research protocols. A 2026 review of 25 co-design studies with people with intellectual disability found only 7 used accessibility standards like Easy Read.

Design implications: easy-read materials, short and frequent sessions, familiar places, and fair pay.

A great example is Spencer González and colleagues. For three years they worked with an advisory panel of adults with intellectual disability, paid like staff. They used easy-read text, adult pictures rather than childish ones, story-based play, and polished prototypes, because rough wireframes confused people.

The third disability theme is technosolutionism: the belief that a new technology will fix the problem. Hassan's 2024 review of game accessibility makes the pattern concrete. Indigenous · Sovereignty

Data sovereignty is rarely addressed.

In a critical review by Vigil-Hayes and colleagues, only 4 of 84 Indigenous health-technology studies engaged with who owns and controls community data. Historical trauma, from colonization and harmful research, shapes trust.

Design implications: community control of data, results shared back, and a community advisory board.

Prior work: Maar et al. 2019 (DREAM-GLOBAL), six practices for cultural safety; 98% would recommend the program.
Maar et al. 2019, building & keeping the research relationship

Now the Indigenous branch. First, sovereignty. A critical review by Vigil-Hayes and colleagues found only 4 of 84 Indigenous health-technology studies engaged with data sovereignty: who owns and controls the community's data. And historical trauma, from colonization and harmful research, shapes the trust any study depends on.

Design implications: the community controls its data, following the CARE principles, and results go back. And a community advisory board guides the questions and approaches.

The example is Maar and colleagues, from DREAM-GLOBAL with First Nations communities. This figure is how they built and kept research relationships before, during, and after the trial. 98 percent of participants would recommend the program.

Indigenous · Cultural Grounding

Programs work when they build on culture.

42.7%
of the hula group reached a healthy blood pressure, vs 21.2% of the comparison group, among Native Hawaiian adults (hula randomized trial)

Design implications: start from what the community values; let the community decide what is shared.

Prior work (NAU): Vigil-Hayes et al. 2021 · ARORA, advisory board set the design values (the butterfly; avoid coyote and lightning).
Vigil-Hayes et al. 2021, ARORA, designed with Navajo & Hopi youth

The second Indigenous theme is cultural grounding: programs work when built into what a community already does. The strongest evidence is the hula trial with 263 Native Hawaiian adults: 42.7 percent reached healthy blood pressure, against 21.2. I use it because it is the only randomized trial of a cultural movement practice for blood pressure. The lesson is the approach, not the dance. Navajo Healthy Stores, run by local Navajo-speaking health workers, shows the same.

Design implications: start from what the community values, and the community decides what is shared, because some games and stories are only for certain seasons.

The great local example is ARORA, by Vigil-Hayes and colleagues at NAU, with Navajo and Hopi youth in Flagstaff. An advisory board set the design values: the butterfly as a positive symbol, and no coyote or lightning.

Serious Games and Play

Most health games are closed systems.

Of the 22 games and playful tools for hypertension in this review, nearly all are closed, structured games; open, situated play is rare. Effects are real but short: eSMART-HD cut systolic pressure about 7 mmHg, yet MyHeartMate kept only 27% of players after 30 days.

Design implications: room for open, social play in daily life, and engagement that lasts beyond the first weeks.

Framework: Duval 2022, serious games are closed; serious play stays open, so people shape it to their lives. Chart: Hickman et al. 2015.
Hickman et al. 2015 · Fig 1, systolic BP, game group (139.9 → 132.8 mmHg)

On the theory side, first: serious games and play. A serious game is a game with a health aim besides fun. Of the 22 games and playful tools for hypertension in this review, nearly all are closed, structured games or simple gamification; open, situated play is rare. The trials are mixed: eSMART-HD, where adults practice a medical visit, lowered systolic pressure about 7 points in the game group, but the difference from control was not significant and the study was small. MyHeartMate kept only 27 percent of its players after 30 days.

Design implications: leave room for open, social play that fits daily life, and design for engagement that lasts.

The framework comes from Duval: serious games for health are closed systems; serious play stays open, so people can shape it to their own lives.

Participatory, Community-Based Research

Few studies design with the community.

About 13 studies designed with the community: 7 with Indigenous communities, 6 with people with disabilities. The two sets barely overlap, and workshops can themselves exclude people.

Design implications: fair pay, respect for community assets, community goals, and support that continues after funding.

Prior work: Lumbee game trial (Radhakrishnan et al. 2025), a paid community committee shaped it and removed a casino theme. It excludes people who use a walker, exactly the gap for this population.
Radhakrishnan et al. 2025, Lumbee sensor-controlled hypertension game

Second: participatory, community-based research. About 13 studies designed with the community, 7 with Indigenous communities and 6 with people with disabilities, and the two sets barely overlap. And Harrington warns that design workshops can themselves be an affluent and privileged activity.

Design implications: pay people, value what the community already has, let the community set the goals, and stay after the funding ends.

The example is the Lumbee game trial, the one hypertension game built with a Native tribe. A paid community committee shaped it, and removed a casino-themed game. But it excludes people who use a walker or have sensory impairments, which is exactly the gap for this population.

Wicked Problems and Research through Design

A health game is a wicked problem, so you learn by building.

No single definition; solutions are better or worse, not right or wrong. Research through design learns by making.

Study, Situated Play Design (Duval 2022): food traditions from many cultures studied as starting points for playful design.

Duval 2022 · Altarriba Bertran et al. 2019

Third: wicked problems and research through design. No paper in my search used these words, so this comes from the theory literature. Duval calls designing a motivating health game a wicked problem: no single definition, and answers are better or worse, not right or wrong. Research through design answers this by learning through making.

Design implications: find the play people already do and build on it; and treat the platform as a probe to learn with, as well as a product.

The example is Situated Play Design. In this workshop, food traditions from many cultures became play potentials, starting points for playful design.

Limitations

  • A scoping review maps the literature; it does not appraise study quality or pool effect sizes.
  • Three databases, PubMed, ACM DL, Google Scholar, English only, searched once (7 Sep 2026); some work is missed.
  • Screening and coding were done by a single reviewer; the thematic hierarchy is one defensible reading, not the only one.
  • The counts show where attention sits, not what works, they are not evidence of effect.

The limitations. A scoping review maps the literature; it does not appraise study quality or pool effects. I searched three databases, in English, once, so some work is missed. Screening and coding were by a single reviewer, so the hierarchy is one defensible reading. And the counts show where attention sits, not what works.

CardioCare Quest Today

A game for each factor, moving for exercise, still for measuring.

One game per factor, built in Flutter + Firebase with story games in Twine, works offline. Not yet deployed; next is accessibility work with players with disabilities and a community data plan.

What is CardioCare Quest today? A game for each factor: Dog Walking for exercise, DASH Diet for diet, Heart Smarts for education, Bingo Bash for medication, where a dose can be marked caregiver-assisted, and Vascular Village for measuring, which uses slow breathing before a calm, seated reading. That covers both sides: moving for exercise, still for measuring.

On the left is the in-app catalog, one game per factor; on the right is the Dog Walking exercise game, a GPS-tracked walk. It is built in Flutter and Firebase, with story games in Twine, and it works offline. It is not deployed yet; the next steps are accessibility work with players with disabilities, and a data plan with the community.

Conclusion

A mapped field, one empty intersection, and a way forward.

  • Across 67 studies, playful technology for hypertension rarely reaches Indigenous communities and people with disabilities, and no study brings a game to both.
  • The review turns that pattern into design requirements traced to the evidence: move for exercise, calm for measuring, culturally grounded, community-owned, open and situated play, designed with disabled players.
  • CardioCare Quest and the Hozhoni studies are a first step; the work ahead is to design playful blood-pressure care with urban Diné adults with disabilities.

To conclude. Across 67 studies, playful technology for hypertension rarely reaches Indigenous communities or people with disabilities, and no study brings a game to both. The review turns that into design requirements traced to the evidence.
