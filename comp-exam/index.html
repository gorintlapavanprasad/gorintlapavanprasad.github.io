What raises the risk? Two studies show the same thing.

(click) Lin and colleagues checked health records of 833 adults with disabilities in Taiwan. People who were overweight or obese had about seven times the odds of hypertension. Weight was the biggest risk.

(click) Goldfield and colleagues asked 282 overweight teenagers in Canada about screen time. TV and computer use did not affect blood pressure. Only video gaming while sitting was linked to higher blood pressure.

(click) So for design: a game should get people moving, not keep them sitting.

The first theme is caregiver support. Many people with disabilities take part with a caregiver. This help can go two ways.

(click) Caro and colleagues changed a Kinect game called FroggyBobby for 10 adults with developmental disabilities. In the photo, a player reaches up to move the frog. In the first session, players needed help 14.4 times. By the third session, only 2.7 times. Their skills grew.

(click) Bircanin and colleagues worked for two years with adults with severe intellectual disability in Australia. They used Active Support: give only the help that is needed, and let the person choose. The left photo shows a helper guiding a hand on the device.

(click) But Sum and colleagues warn about a risk. The helper can start speaking and acting for the person.

(click) So for design: the helper supports, and the player decides. Even pressing the cuff button counts as taking part.

The third theme is technosolutionism. This is the belief that new technology will fix the problem.

Most accessible technology work still treats disability as something to fix.

(click) Hassan reviewed 162 papers on accessible games from 2016 to 2020. Only 13 used disability studies theory. The rest treated disability as a problem.

(click) Sum and colleagues say the same. They describe a push to treat disabled people as problems that need fixing.

(click) Ymous and colleagues wrote a paper with seven disabled researchers. They describe how research wrote about them as burdens. The title is "I am just terrified of my future."

(click) For design: first check that the problem is real for the community. And respect people who choose not to take part.

The first Indigenous theme is data sovereignty. This means: who owns and controls the community's data. Few studies discuss it.

(click) Vigil-Hayes and colleagues here at NAU reviewed Indigenous health technology studies from 2012 to 2022. Only 4 out of 84 talked about who owns the data.

(click) Kaholokula and colleagues ran a trial called Ola Hou i ka Hula. It was a hula dance program with 263 Native Hawaiian adults.

First, what is a serious game?

(click) Wattanasoontorn and colleagues say a serious game has two goals: to be fun, and to teach a skill or improve health. They studied 108 health games.

(click) Duval describes two kinds. Serious games for health are closed. They have fixed rules, and the game controls everything.

(click) Serious play for health also has a health goal, but it stays open. People can shape it to fit their own lives, starting from play they already do.

(click) So, which kind are the hypertension games, and do they work?

Serious Games and Play

Most health games are closed systems.

  • Hickman et al. 2015, eSMART-HD[1]: a virtual clinic where adults with hypertension practise talking with their provider.
  • The figure: systolic pressure in the game group fell about 7 mmHg, but not significantly versus control.
  • Gallagher et al. 2023, MyHeartMate[2]: players care for a heart avatar. Almost everyone engaged at first, but only 27% kept going past 30 days.
  • Both are closed games. Of the 22 hypertension games and playful tools, nearly all are closed; open, situated play is rare[3].
→
Design implications: room for open, social play in daily life, and engagement that lasts beyond the first weeks.

Here is what the hypertension games show.

(click) Hickman and colleagues built eSMART-HD. It is a virtual clinic where adults with hypertension practice talking with their doctor.

(click) This figure shows the game group. The top number fell from about 140 to 133, about 7 points. But compared with the control group, the difference was not significant.

(click) Gallagher and colleagues tested MyHeartMate. Players take care of a heart character. Almost everyone used it at first, but only 27 percent were still using it after 30 days.

Participatory, Community-Based Research

Few studies design with the community.

  • About 13 studies designed with the community: 7 with Indigenous communities, 6 with people with disabilities.
  • The two sets barely overlap, and workshops can themselves exclude people.
  • Radhakrishnan et al. 2025[1]: a sensor-controlled hypertension game, in a randomized trial with the Lumbee Tribe; a paid committee shaped it and removed a casino theme.
  • The screens: steps move the avatar up a trail, and coins buy regalia in the shop.
  • It excludes people who use a walker, exactly the gap for this population.
→
Design implications: fair pay, respect for community assets, community goals, and support that continues after funding.
[1]Radhakrishnan et al. 2025
Radhakrishnan et al. 2025, Lumbee sensor-controlled hypertension game

The second theory theme is designing with the community. Few studies do this.

(click) About 13 studies did: 7 with Indigenous communities and 6 with people with disabilities.

(click) These two groups of studies barely overlap. And workshops themselves can leave some people out.

(click) One example is Radhakrishnan and colleagues' Lumbee game. It is a step-based hypertension game, tested in a trial with the Lumbee Tribe. A paid community committee shaped it and removed a casino theme.

(click) In these screens, real steps move the player up a trail, and coins buy cultural items in the shop.

(click) But it does not allow people who use a walker. So people with mobility disabilities cannot take part. Like the other studies, it focuses on one group.

(click) For design: pay people, let the community set the goals, respect what they already have, and stay after the funding ends.

Wicked Problems and Research through Design

A health game is a wicked problem.

  • Duval 2022[1], a dissertation on playful health technology: it has no single definition.
  • Solutions are better or worse rather than right or wrong.
  • Research through design learns by making[1].
  • Altarriba Bertran et al. 2019[2], Situated Play Design: start from the play already in people's lives.
  • The photo: a workshop where food traditions from many cultures became starting points for play.
→

Conclusion

The gap, and the way forward.

  • Playful technology for hypertension rarely reaches Indigenous communities or people with disabilities, and no study reaches both.
  • The review reports design requirements derived from that evidence: movement and calm, support that fades, cultural grounding with community-owned data, and open, situated play.
  • The way forward: design playful blood-pressure care with urban Diné adults with disabilities.

Backup · Selection Criteria

Inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Include (all three)

  1. A playful or technological intervention
  2. Targets hypertension or blood pressure
  3. Involves an Indigenous or disability population, or a method for building with one

Exclude

  • "Game" only as a figure of speech (e.g., "a game changer")
  • No tie to hypertension
  • Teaches clinicians, not self-management

Included: journal and conference papers, theses, preprints.

Backup. The selection criteria. To be included, a study had to meet all three: a playful or technological intervention; a focus on hypertension or blood pressure; and an Indigenous or disability population, or a method for building with one.
