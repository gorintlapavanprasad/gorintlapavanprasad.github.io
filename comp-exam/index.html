What is hypertension?

Hypertension is the medical name for high blood pressure. It means the pressure of the blood inside the blood vessels stays higher than normal for a long time.

Blood pressure has two numbers.

• The upper number is systolic: the pressure when the heart beats.

• The lower number is diastolic: the pressure when the heart rests between beats.

(click) Normal: systolic is below 120 and diastolic is below 80.

(click) Elevated: systolic is 120 to 129, and diastolic is below 80.

(click) Stage 1 hypertension: systolic is 130 to 139, or diastolic is 80 to 89.

(click) Stage 2 hypertension: systolic is 140 or higher, or diastolic is 90 or higher.

(click) So hypertension starts at 130 over 80. That is the threshold in the guideline.

(click) In 2016, Harmon and colleagues studied Navajo adults.

• They noted that cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of non-accidental death among Navajo people.

(click) In 2023, Eberly and colleagues studied the Medicare records of about 220,000 American Indian and Alaska Native adults aged 65 and older, from 2015 to 2019.

• 72.2 percent of them had hypertension.

(click) In 2023, Rawal and colleagues studied 8,497 American Indian and Alaska Native adults. They estimated how much heart disease and stroke is linked to each risk factor.

• Hypertension was linked to 42 percent, the largest share of any factor.

Here is what each factor means.

(click) Diet means choosing healthy foods every day.

• One well-known eating plan is DASH: Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension.

• It is built on fruits, vegetables, whole grains and low-fat dairy.

(click) In 2023, Sinclair and colleagues noted that DASH had not been tested with American Indian and Alaska Native adults.

• So they planned a DASH trial with 370 urban Native adults.

• Community dietitians added traditional foods, such as corn, salmon, beans and squash.

(click) Exercise means moving the body regularly to stay healthy.

• For Navajo people, this can be walking, farming, herding and dancing.

(click) In 2020, Breathett and colleagues reported two findings.

• Only 14.7 percent of American Indian and Alaska Native adults get the recommended physical activity.

• Community exercise classes for American Indian elders lowered systolic blood pressure.

(click) Medication means taking medicine as prescribed.

(click) In 2003, Rhoades and Buchwald studied urban Native elders with hypertension in Seattle.

• 83 percent were taking medicine.

• But only 36 percent had their blood pressure under control.

(click) Education means learning about hypertension and how to control it.

(click) In 2015, Kaholokula and colleagues worked with 55 Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander adults with high blood pressure.

• Along with hula classes, they taught them about symptoms, medicines, healthy eating, and activity and emotions.

• After 12 weeks, their systolic blood pressure fell by 18.3 points.

(click) In hypertension self-management, measurement means checking your own blood pressure regularly and accurately.

(click) In 1997, Percy and colleagues measured the blood pressure of Navajo adults and found 97 adults with high blood pressure.

• Among them, 50 knew they had hypertension, and 47 did not.

Closed games or open play?

Closed game

The system sets the rules and measures performance, as in eSMART [1] and Heart Health Mountain [2].

Open play

Players can change the rules; play is built on what people already do in daily life.

Situated Play Design

Three steps: find the play that already happens, design to support it, and test it in daily life [3].

[1] Clochesy et al. 2015[2] Radhakrishnan et al. 2025[3] Altarriba Bertran et al. 2019

A closed game sets the rules and measures how the player performs.

• eSMART and Heart Health Mountain are closed games: the game decides what counts as success.

Open play leaves room for players to change the rules. It builds on what people already do.

Situated Play Design, by Altarriba Bertran, Duval and colleagues, has three steps.

• Find the play that already happens in daily life.

• Design something that supports it.

• Test it in daily life.

PRQ4 asks how these two approaches differ in keeping people engaged.

Why research through design for a wicked problem?

Wicked problem

A problem with no single correct answer; solutions are better or worse, not right or wrong [1].

Research through Design

Making prototypes with the community is how new knowledge is produced [2].

[1] Rittel & Webber 1973[2] Graham, Ikwunne & Duval 2025

Rittel and Webber describe wicked problems: problems with no single correct answer, where each solution is better or worse, not right or wrong.

Hypertension self-management with urban Diné adults with disabilities is such a problem. It depends on culture, place, disability, family and care.

So the work uses research through design. Making prototypes with the community is how new knowledge is produced.
